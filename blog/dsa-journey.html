Learning & engineering practice

My love-hate, ever-evolving journey with data structures and algorithms

By Nikhil Narra May 29, 2025 7 min read

Even after countless hours spent diving into data structures and algorithms, I often feel I have barely scratched the surface. I once treated mastery as a destination. Over time, I learned that it is an ongoing process of discovery, application, and refinement.

Building the foundation

My learning started with arrays, linked lists, stacks, queues, trees, and graphs. Understanding the mechanics mattered, but the more useful shift came when I stopped asking only how a structure worked and started asking when its trade-offs made it the right choice.

From memorization to patterns

Algorithmic thinking became more approachable once I focused on recurring patterns rather than isolated solutions. Sliding windows, two pointers, divide and conquer, graph traversal, and dynamic programming became tools for framing problems instead of answers to memorize.

  • Understand time and space complexity before optimizing.
  • Recognize the constraints that make one pattern fit better than another.
  • Practice explaining the reasoning, not only writing the code.
The most valuable lesson was simple: strong problem solving comes from understanding principles deeply enough to adapt them.

Turning concepts into engineering judgment

The value of data structures and algorithms became clearer when I applied them outside of exercises. In production and research work, the questions are rarely labeled. You have to identify whether the real challenge is latency, throughput, memory, reliability, data shape, or maintainability.

Working with 3D visualization and large datasets reinforced this. Rendering pipelines, scene graphs, benchmarking systems, and search services all depend on choices about how information is organized, traversed, measured, and updated.

The practice that helped me most

  1. Return to the fundamentals regularly.
  2. Practice consistently instead of relying on short bursts.
  3. Implement concepts inside real projects.
  4. Study how production systems use the same ideas at scale.
  5. Write down the trade-offs behind each decision.

The journey continues

Understanding data structures and algorithms is not a box to check. It is a continuing practice of learning, adapting, and improving the quality of the decisions we make as engineers. That unfinished nature is sometimes frustrating, but it is also what keeps the work interesting.
